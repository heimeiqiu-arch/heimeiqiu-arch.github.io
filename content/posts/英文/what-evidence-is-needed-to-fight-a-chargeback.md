When a customer disputes a card payment, a merchant may have an opportunity to challenge the case. However, simply stating that an order was legitimate is rarely enough.

A chargeback response needs evidence that addresses the specific reason for the dispute. The right documents depend on what the customer claims. A delivery dispute requires different records from an unauthorized transaction or a subscription cancellation complaint.

## Start With the Dispute Reason

Before collecting documents, identify the reason code or dispute category shown in the notification. It helps determine what the issuer is questioning: authorization, delivery, processing, billing, cancellation, or product quality.

Do not assume one type of evidence proves everything. A delivery confirmation may help with a non-receipt claim, but it does not necessarily prove that the cardholder authorized the transaction.

## 1. Transaction and Payment Records

Start with the original transaction. Useful records may include the payment reference or transaction ID, order number, transaction date and time, amount and currency, authorization result, settlement information, available authentication results, and refund or reversal records.

These details establish which transaction is being discussed. They are especially important when a customer has placed multiple orders or several payment attempts occurred close together.

For duplicate-payment disputes, include references and timestamps for each relevant attempt. This helps distinguish a completed duplicate charge from a pending authorization or repeated dashboard entry.

## 2. Order Confirmation and Checkout Details

The merchant should be able to show what the customer agreed to purchase. Relevant documents may include the order confirmation, invoice or receipt, product or service description, price and currency displayed at checkout, shipping or service fees, subscription terms, and applicable cancellation and refund terms.

For recurring payments, the evidence should show how the recurring price and billing frequency were disclosed.

A screenshot of the current website may not prove what the customer saw when the transaction occurred. Where possible, preserve dated versions of checkout content, order confirmations, and applicable terms.

## 3. Delivery and Fulfillment Evidence

For physical goods, useful evidence may include shipping confirmation, carrier tracking number, delivery status and timestamp, available proof of delivery, relevant address information, and customer correspondence.

For digital products and services, evidence may include account activation records, download availability logs, service access timestamps, license or subscription activation records, and support messages confirming access.

Be careful not to claim that a digital service was used merely because an account was created. Evidence should accurately reflect what was delivered and what records establish.

## 4. Customer Communication

Customer communication can provide important context. Relevant records include emails confirming the purchase, support tickets, delivery inquiries, cancellation requests, refund discussions, messages acknowledging receipt, and technical support conversations.

Include the complete relevant conversation where possible, rather than selecting a sentence that could be misunderstood. Dates should be visible, and messages should be linked to the correct order or transaction.

If communication occurred through several channels, organize records chronologically.

## 5. Evidence for Unauthorized Transaction Disputes

Unauthorized transaction disputes require particular care. Depending on the transaction and provider requirements, potentially relevant records may include authentication results, available card verification or security checks, transaction and device information, account login records, previous transaction history, delivery or service access records, and customer communication.

No single data point should be treated as conclusive proof unless the applicable rules say so. An IP address or device match may provide context, but it does not automatically establish who personally authorized a transaction.

Follow the provider's evidence instructions and avoid collecting or disclosing unnecessary sensitive information.

## 6. Evidence for Subscription Disputes

Subscription cases often involve consent, renewal, cancellation, and billing.

A useful evidence package may include the original signup date, selected plan, recurring price and billing frequency shown at signup, renewal terms, reminder or renewal notice, cancellation policy, cancellation history, date the service remained available, and any refund or cancellation confirmation.

If the customer cancelled before a renewal, investigate whether the cancellation was processed correctly. The original agreement cannot resolve a dispute if the customer later submitted a valid cancellation request.

## 7. Evidence for Product or Service Quality Disputes

When a customer claims a product was not as described, compare the original offer with what was delivered.

Relevant evidence may include the product listing at the time of purchase, service specifications, order configuration, delivery records, customer acceptance or feedback, support conversations, troubleshooting steps, and refund or replacement offers.

Do not rely only on marketing claims. Demonstrate what the customer was promised and what the merchant provided.

## How to Organize Evidence

A well-organized package is easier to review than a folder of unrelated files.

**01 - Transaction Summary:** A short explanation of the disputed payment and the merchant's position.

**02 - Purchase Records:** The order confirmation, invoice, and relevant checkout information.

**03 - Fulfillment Records:** Delivery confirmation, access logs, or service completion evidence.

**04 - Customer Communication:** Relevant messages arranged by date.

**05 - Additional Payment Records:** Authorization, authentication, refund, and settlement information.

Use clear filenames such as `order-confirmation.pdf` or `delivery-record.pdf`. Avoid vague names like `document1-final-new.pdf`.

## Protect Customer Data

Chargeback evidence may contain personal or financial information. Only submit information that is relevant, permitted, and requested by the provider.

Never include full card numbers, security codes, passwords, or unrelated account details. Follow the provider's instructions for redaction and secure submission.

## Final Thoughts

Good chargeback evidence is accurate, relevant, and organized around the dispute reason.

Businesses should not wait until a dispute arrives to start collecting records. Build a routine that preserves order details, payment references, fulfillment information, and customer communication. A reliable evidence system makes investigations faster and helps merchants respond consistently when a transaction is challenged.
