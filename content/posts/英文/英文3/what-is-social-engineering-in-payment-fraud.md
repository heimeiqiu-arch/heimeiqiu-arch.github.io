Social engineering is a type of fraud in which criminals manipulate people into revealing information, approving transactions or performing actions that benefit the attacker.

Instead of relying only on technical vulnerabilities, social engineering exploits trust, urgency, fear or confusion. In payment environments, criminals may impersonate banks, merchants, payment providers, customer support agents or colleagues.

Their objective may be to obtain card details, account passwords, verification codes or approval for an unauthorised transaction.

## Common social engineering techniques

**Phishing emails.** A criminal sends an email that appears to come from a legitimate payment provider. It may claim that an account has been restricted or that a transaction requires verification.

**Fake customer support.** An attacker pretends to be a support representative and asks the victim to provide sensitive information.

**Impersonation calls.** A fraudster may claim to be from a bank's fraud department and create urgency around a supposed suspicious transaction.

**Fake payment requests.** A criminal sends an invoice or payment link that appears to come from a known supplier or business contact.

**Business email compromise.** An attacker compromises or impersonates a business email account and asks an employee to change payment details or send funds to a different account.

## Why these scams work

Social engineering attacks often create pressure. A message may say an account will be closed unless the recipient acts immediately. A caller may claim that a suspicious transaction can only be cancelled by sharing a verification code.

These techniques reduce the victim's opportunity to verify the request. Attackers may also use information gathered from social media, previous data breaches or public business records to make messages more convincing.

## How to recognise a payment scam

Warning signs include requests for passwords or one-time codes, unexpected payment links, pressure to act immediately, requests to move money to a supposedly safer account, unusual changes to supplier details, lookalike email addresses, requests to install remote-access software and instructions to bypass normal approval procedures.

A professional-looking logo or familiar company name does not prove that a message is genuine.

## How consumers can protect themselves

Never disclose passwords, PINs or one-time verification codes to someone who contacts you unexpectedly.

If a message claims to come from a bank or payment provider, open the official app or website independently. Do not use contact details supplied in a suspicious message; find the organisation's official support channel instead.

Before approving a transaction, check the recipient, amount and payment purpose carefully. If someone pressures you to act immediately, pause and verify the request.

## How businesses can reduce the risk

Establish clear payment approval procedures. Changes to supplier bank details should be independently verified using a trusted contact method already on record.

Employees should receive training on phishing, impersonation and fraudulent payment requests. High-value payments should require appropriate authorisation rather than relying on a single email instruction.

Protect email accounts with multi-factor authentication and monitor unusual login activity.

## What to do if you have been scammed

Contact your bank or payment provider immediately if you have shared sensitive information or authorised a suspicious transaction. Change compromised passwords and secure related accounts.

Preserve messages, emails, telephone numbers and transaction records. These may help the provider investigate the incident.

If business funds were transferred, notify the relevant finance and security teams immediately. Time can be critical when attempting to recover a fraudulent payment.

## Final thoughts

Social engineering attacks exploit human trust rather than relying solely on technical weaknesses. Verify unexpected requests independently, protect authentication information and avoid making payment decisions under pressure.

For people managing multiple online payment accounts, keeping payment methods organised and reviewing transactions regularly can improve visibility.
