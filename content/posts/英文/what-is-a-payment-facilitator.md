# What Is a Payment Facilitator (PayFac)? How the Model Works

A business that wants to accept card payments may need to work with a payment provider, complete onboarding checks, and arrange the services required to process transactions.

For some businesses, a payment facilitator can make this process more integrated. Often called a PayFac, a payment facilitator offers a model through which eligible businesses can accept payments under the facilitator's broader payment arrangement, subject to the relevant network rules and acquiring relationships.

Understanding this model helps merchants compare payment providers and evaluate how onboarding, compliance, fees, and settlement responsibilities are handled.

## What Is a Payment Facilitator?

A payment facilitator is a provider that enables eligible submerchants to accept payments through an arrangement supported by an acquiring institution and the relevant payment system.

Rather than every small business arranging all aspects of a traditional merchant setup independently, a submerchant may onboard through the facilitator's platform. The facilitator manages defined parts of the relationship and payment experience under its contractual and regulatory obligations.

The exact legal structure, responsibilities, and terminology depend on the market, acquiring arrangement, and applicable card-network rules.

Not every company that calls itself a payment platform is necessarily a payment facilitator in the formal sense. Businesses should verify the actual service model and the entities involved.

## How Does the PayFac Model Work?

A simplified payment-facilitator arrangement may involve several steps.

### 1. The Business Applies

A business submits information about its ownership, products, expected transaction activity, and other details requested during onboarding.

### 2. The Facilitator Reviews the Business

The facilitator performs the checks required under its policies and relevant obligations. These may include identity verification, business validation, risk assessment, and review of the products or services being sold.

Approval is not automatic. Some business types or transaction patterns may be unsupported.

### 3. Payment Acceptance Is Enabled

If approved, the business receives access to the relevant payment functionality. The technical setup may include checkout tools, APIs, hosted payment pages, or other integrations.

### 4. Customers Make Payments

Customers pay through the merchant's supported checkout. Transactions are handled through the facilitator's payment arrangement and relevant processing infrastructure.

### 5. Funds Are Reconciled and Paid Out

The facilitator and its partners handle payment records and fund movement according to the applicable agreements. The timing, deductions, reporting, and payout conditions depend on the service terms.

This overview is simplified. Actual responsibilities may be divided among the facilitator, acquirer, processor, card network, and other providers.

## What Is the Difference Between a PayFac and a Traditional Merchant Account Setup?

The main difference is often how the merchant relationship is structured and how onboarding and payment services are delivered.

In a traditional setup, a merchant may work directly with an acquiring institution or payment provider to establish its own merchant arrangement. The details vary by provider and market.

Under a PayFac model, eligible businesses may onboard through the facilitator as submerchants within the facilitator's broader arrangement.

The PayFac model can offer a more integrated experience, particularly for platforms that serve many smaller businesses. However, it does not remove all onboarding, risk, or compliance requirements.

Businesses should compare the actual contract, underwriting process, payout terms, and support responsibilities rather than assuming that one model is always simpler or less expensive.

## Why Do Platforms Use a Payment Facilitator Model?

A platform serving many businesses may want to offer payments as part of its overall product.

For example, a software platform might serve appointment-based businesses, online sellers, or service providers. Integrating payment acceptance can let these businesses manage sales and related records within a more unified workflow.

A PayFac model may help the platform coordinate onboarding and payment functionality across its eligible users.

However, providing payment services introduces responsibilities. The platform must understand its contractual role, applicable regulatory obligations, risk controls, and arrangements with acquiring partners.

## What Are the Potential Benefits of a PayFac?

### More Integrated Onboarding

A facilitator may provide a standardized application and setup process for eligible businesses. This can reduce the need to coordinate multiple providers independently.

The actual onboarding time still depends on the completeness of the application, risk review, business type, and provider procedures.

### Unified Payment Tools

Some facilitators offer payment acceptance, transaction reporting, refunds, and related functions through one interface.

The scope of these features differs by provider, so merchants should confirm what is included.

### Platform-Level Integration

For businesses using a software platform, embedded payment functionality may reduce the need to manage separate payment interfaces.

This can be useful when payment activity needs to connect with bookings, orders, invoices, or other business records.

### Support for Smaller Businesses

A facilitator may provide a route to payment acceptance for eligible businesses that prefer a packaged service instead of coordinating every part of the payment arrangement themselves.

Eligibility remains subject to the provider's requirements and risk policies.

## What Should Merchants Watch Out For?

An integrated setup can be convenient, but businesses should understand the details before committing.

### Fees and Additional Charges

Review transaction pricing, monthly charges, refund fees, dispute-related costs, currency conversion charges, and any other applicable fees.

A simple headline rate does not necessarily represent the full cost.

### Payout Timing and Reserves

Check how payouts are scheduled, what may delay them, and under what circumstances funds can be held or reserves applied.

These terms can have a meaningful effect on cash flow.

### Account Review and Restrictions

Payment providers may review business activity over time. Changes in product type, transaction volume, dispute levels, or other risk indicators can lead to additional review under the service terms.

Merchants should understand the provider's acceptable-use policies and the process for resolving account questions.

### Data Access and Portability

Find out how to access transaction records, export reports, and retrieve relevant information if the business changes providers.

Good recordkeeping can make reconciliation and a future migration easier.

### Customer Support

Confirm who handles questions about onboarding, failed payments, refunds, disputes, and payouts. The facilitator may coordinate with partner institutions, so understanding the support process is important.

## How Does a PayFac Differ From a Payment Gateway?

A payment gateway is commonly a technology service that connects a checkout interface to payment infrastructure.

A payment facilitator is a broader service model centered on enabling eligible submerchants to accept payments under the relevant acquiring and contractual arrangements.

A provider may offer gateway functionality as part of its services, but the terms describe different concepts. One concerns a payment-technology function; the other concerns how payment acceptance and merchant relationships are organized.

When evaluating a provider, ask whether it supplies gateway technology, processing services, acquiring arrangements, PayFac services, or a combination of these.

## Is a PayFac Suitable for Every Business?

No. The right setup depends on the business's size, sales model, risk profile, technical needs, target markets, and commercial requirements.

A small business may prefer an integrated service with straightforward onboarding and reporting. A larger merchant with specialized needs may prefer more direct control over its acquiring relationships and payment infrastructure.

A platform serving many submerchants may have different requirements from a single retailer.

Before choosing a provider, compare onboarding eligibility, total cost, supported payment methods, payout conditions, reporting, support, and the process for moving to another service if needed.

## Do Virtual Cards Work Differently Under a PayFac Model?

A virtual card is a card credential format, while a PayFac is a payment-acceptance model for merchants. They refer to different parts of the payment ecosystem.

A business can use virtual cards for eligible business purchases while separately using a payment facilitator to accept payments from its own customers. The applicable terms, transaction eligibility, and payment flows depend on the products and providers involved.

For users exploring virtual or physical cards for online purchases and business expenses, [CBPayments](https://www.cbpayments.cc/) provides information about available card options. Review the specific card terms and supported uses before relying on a card for a particular expense.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a PayFac eliminate underwriting?

No. A facilitator generally has onboarding and risk-review responsibilities. The exact process varies, but merchants should expect to provide accurate business information and satisfy the applicable eligibility requirements.

### Is a PayFac the same as a payment processor?

No. A payment processor handles transaction-processing functions, while a PayFac describes a model for enabling eligible submerchants to accept payments. A provider may offer both types of services or rely on partners.

### Can a PayFac delay a merchant's payout?

Payout timing depends on the service agreement and payment arrangement. Providers may apply processing schedules, reviews, reserves, or other contractual conditions. Merchants should review the terms and contact the provider when a payout is unclear.

### Can a business change from a PayFac to another payment provider?

Potentially, but the process depends on the contracts, technical integration, data-export options, and the new provider's onboarding requirements. Businesses should plan for reconciliation, customer communication, and continuity of payment acceptance.

## Final Thoughts

A payment facilitator can provide an integrated way for eligible businesses to accept card payments through a broader payment arrangement. The model can simplify some parts of onboarding and payment integration, particularly for platforms serving many smaller businesses.

However, convenience should not replace careful review. Merchants should understand the facilitator's role, fees, payout terms, risk policies, support process, and the entities involved in handling their payments.

Comparing the complete service arrangement is the best way to decide whether a PayFac fits your business.
