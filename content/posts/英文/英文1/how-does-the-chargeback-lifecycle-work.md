A chargeback is not a single event. It is a sequence of steps involving the cardholder, issuing institution, card network, acquiring provider, and merchant.

Understanding the **chargeback lifecycle** helps businesses respond within the correct timeframes and organise the information needed to investigate a dispute. The precise process varies by network, dispute category, and regional rules, but the following overview explains the main stages.

## What is the chargeback lifecycle?

The chargeback lifecycle is the sequence of procedures through which a disputed card transaction is raised, reviewed, challenged, and resolved. It begins when a cardholder contacts their issuer about a transaction and may continue through several formal dispute stages.

Not every case reaches the final escalation stage. Some disputes are resolved early, accepted by the merchant, or closed under applicable procedures.

## Stage 1: The cardholder questions a transaction

The process often starts when a cardholder does not recognise a transaction or believes something went wrong. Concerns may include an unauthorised purchase, missing goods, an incorrect amount, duplicate processing, or a refund that has not appeared.

The cardholder contacts the issuer or follows its dispute process.

## Stage 2: The issuer reviews the claim

The issuer examines the cardholder's information and determines how the issue should be handled. Depending on the circumstances and applicable rules, it may request additional information or initiate a formal chargeback.

The customer's initial description is not necessarily the final determination.

## Stage 3: The chargeback is submitted

If the dispute qualifies, the issuer submits the chargeback through the relevant network process. The acquiring side receives the information and passes the notification to the merchant, generally including transaction details and the dispute category.

## Stage 4: The merchant investigates

The merchant reviews the disputed transaction. Useful records may include the original order, invoice, transaction details, delivery confirmation, service-access information, authentication records, customer communications, and refund history.

The merchant should identify the exact allegation before deciding whether to accept or challenge the dispute.

## Stage 5: The merchant responds

If the merchant accepts the dispute, it follows the provider's acceptance procedure. If it challenges the dispute and the case is eligible, it prepares supporting evidence for representment through its acquirer.

The response must meet the applicable deadline and documentation requirements.

## Stage 6: The issuer reviews the response

The issuer evaluates the merchant's response under the relevant rules. The dispute may be resolved at this stage, or the issuer may maintain its position. The result depends on evidence, procedural requirements, and the network framework.

## Stage 7: Further stages may follow

Some disputes may proceed to pre-arbitration or arbitration if the applicable rules allow further escalation. These stages may involve additional submissions, deadlines, and fees.

Not every case is eligible for every stage, and escalation should be considered carefully.

## What affects the lifecycle?

**Network rules:** Each card network has its own dispute procedures and requirements.

**Dispute category:** Different allegations may require different evidence and follow different paths.

**Transaction type:** Card-present, card-not-present, recurring, and other transaction types may have different considerations.

**Regional requirements:** Local laws and regulatory rules may affect certain dispute rights and procedures.

**Provider workflow:** An acquirer or processor may set operational deadlines earlier than the final network deadline.

## Manage the lifecycle effectively

Build a consistent process for receiving notifications, identifying transactions, assigning an owner, gathering evidence, deciding whether to accept or challenge, and recording outcomes.

A centralised dispute log can track the reason code, amount, transaction date, response deadline, evidence status, and final result.

Analyse recurring patterns too. Repeated complaints about delivery, billing descriptors, or subscription cancellations may indicate operational problems that should be corrected.

## Why prevention matters

Responding to chargebacks is only one part of dispute management. Clear billing descriptors, accurate product descriptions, reliable fulfilment, accessible customer support, transparent cancellation terms, and timely refunds can help reduce avoidable disputes.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does every chargeback reach arbitration?

No. Many disputes are resolved or closed during earlier stages.

### How long does the lifecycle take?

There is no universal duration. The timeline depends on the network, dispute category, provider deadlines, and whether the case is escalated.

### Can a merchant stop a chargeback after it starts?

Options depend on the stage and applicable procedures. Contact the acquirer or payment provider rather than assuming that a normal refund or message will automatically close the dispute.
