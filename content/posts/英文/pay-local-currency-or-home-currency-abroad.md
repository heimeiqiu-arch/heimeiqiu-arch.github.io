At a checkout counter abroad, a card terminal may ask whether you want to pay in the local currency or your home currency. The home-currency amount is easier to understand, but it may include a less favorable conversion rate. The right choice depends on the rate offered, your card's fee terms, and how the transaction is processed.

## Paying in local currency

When you choose the merchant's local currency, the merchant submits the transaction in that currency. If your card account uses another currency, the card's normal conversion arrangements generally determine the amount that appears on your statement.

For example, a visitor in France might pay EUR 80 with a USD-denominated card. The transaction is submitted in EUR and converted under the card's applicable rules. The final USD amount may differ from a public currency calculator because of rate source, timing, or issuer fees.

## Paying in your home currency

If the terminal offers a home-currency amount, it may be using Dynamic Currency Conversion (DCC). The DCC provider calculates the conversion at checkout and displays the amount before you confirm. This can provide certainty about the displayed amount, but the exchange rate may include a markup.

A familiar currency is not automatically a cheaper currency. Compare the offered amount and rate with the conversion method and fees on your card.

## A simple comparison

Imagine a EUR 100 purchase. A reference conversion might indicate USD 110, while the terminal offers DCC at USD 115. If the card has no applicable foreign transaction fee, the local-currency route may be less expensive in this simplified example. But if the card charges a foreign transaction fee, include that cost in the comparison. Actual rates and fees vary; the example is not a prediction of a real transaction.

## A checkout checklist

1. Confirm the merchant entered the correct local-currency amount.
2. Read the home-currency amount and exchange rate shown on the terminal.
3. Look for a disclosed conversion markup.
4. Know whether your card charges a foreign transaction fee.
5. Do not select a currency just because the number looks familiar.
6. Keep the receipt showing the currency you selected.

If the terminal does not disclose enough information to compare the offers, paying in local currency is often a sensible default because it avoids accepting an unknown merchant-provided rate. It does not guarantee that no issuer fee will apply.

## If the receipt shows the wrong currency

Check the receipt before leaving when possible. If it records a currency you did not knowingly choose, ask the merchant to explain the transaction. Keep the receipt and contact the card issuer if the payment appears inconsistent with your authorization. Do not assume every unexpected statement amount is DCC; taxes, deposits, tips, and settlement adjustments can also affect the final amount.

## Prepare before traveling

Review foreign transaction fees on your cards, carry a backup payment method, and keep official issuer support details available. For hotels and car rentals, understand that a temporary authorization may differ from the final bill. Business travelers should follow their company's currency and receipt policy so that expenses can be reconciled later.

## Final thoughts

Local currency is a sensible starting point when a DCC offer is unclear, but the cheapest choice depends on the complete rate and fee comparison. Understand your card terms before traveling and retain transaction receipts.
