# How to Handle Currency Differences When Invoicing Overseas Clients

Freelancers who work with international clients often need to decide which currency to use when quoting prices and issuing invoices.

A designer may price projects in USD, receive payments in EUR, and maintain personal or business accounts in another currency. A consultant may agree on a fixed project fee but discover that the final amount received is different because of exchange-rate movements and transfer fees.

These differences can create confusion if the payment terms are not clear.

The solution is to distinguish between the currency used for pricing, the currency shown on the invoice, and the currency ultimately received.

## 1. Understand the Three Main Currency Types

International freelance payments can involve three separate currencies.

**Pricing currency** is the currency used to calculate and negotiate the project fee. For example, a freelancer may quote USD 2,000 for a website project.

**Invoice currency** is the currency displayed on the invoice and used to define the amount the client owes. The invoice may use the same currency as the original quote, or the parties may agree to a different currency.

**Settlement currency** is the currency ultimately credited to the freelancer's receiving account. If the receiving account does not support the invoice currency, conversion may occur before or during settlement.

Keeping these three concepts separate prevents many payment misunderstandings.

## 2. Choose an Invoice Currency Before Work Begins

The invoice currency should be agreed upon before the project starts.

Consider the client's preferred payment currency, the freelancer's available receiving methods, supported currencies in the relevant countries, conversion costs, accounting requirements, and expected project duration.

Using the client's local currency may make budgeting easier for the client.

Using a stable working currency may make pricing more predictable for the freelancer.

Neither option is universally better.

The important point is that both parties understand the currency and the amount before work begins.

## 3. Decide Who Pays Currency Conversion Costs

Currency conversion can reduce the amount received by a freelancer.

For example, a client agrees to pay EUR 1,000, but the freelancer's receiving method converts the funds into another currency.

The final credited amount may depend on the exchange rate, provider margin, and applicable fees.

The invoice or contract should explain who is responsible for conversion costs.

Possible arrangements include the client paying all sender-side charges, the freelancer accepting recipient-side fees, both parties accepting their own provider charges, or the parties agreeing to a specific settlement currency.

Avoid promising that the freelancer will receive an exact converted amount unless the payment arrangement actually guarantees it.

## 4. Understand Exchange-Rate Changes

Exchange rates can change between the date a project is quoted and the date payment is received.

This matters especially for long projects.

Suppose a freelancer quotes a project in one currency but pays regular business expenses in another. If the exchange rate moves significantly during the project, the freelancer's actual earnings may change.

For longer contracts, consider agreeing on a fixed invoice currency, the date used to determine conversion, whether the price can be reviewed after a specified period, and how exchange-rate changes are handled.

Do not change an already agreed invoice amount without a clear contractual basis and communication with the client.

## 5. Make the Invoice Currency Impossible to Misunderstand

Every international invoice should identify the currency clearly.

Use a recognizable currency code such as USD, EUR, GBP, or JPY rather than relying only on a currency symbol.

Some symbols are used by multiple currencies.

For example, the dollar symbol may refer to different currencies depending on the country.

A clear invoice should show the currency code, amount payable, invoice date, due date, payment instructions, and any agreed fee arrangements.

If the invoice is for USD 2,000, write the currency code alongside the amount.

This is safer than displaying only "$2,000."

## 6. Record the Actual Conversion Rate

When the payment arrives in a different currency, record the actual settlement details.

Useful information includes the original invoice amount, original invoice currency, amount sent by the client, amount credited, receiving currency, conversion rate, provider fees, date of conversion, and transaction reference.

Do not assume that the exchange rate shown on a public currency website is the rate applied by a bank or payment provider.

The actual transaction statement is the more relevant record for reconciling the payment.

For accounting or tax reporting, follow the rules applicable to the freelancer's jurisdiction and use a consistent documented method.

## 7. Investigate Differences Between Invoice and Receipt

If the amount received is lower than the invoice, identify the reason before contacting the client.

Possible explanations include sender-side fees, intermediary bank deductions, recipient-side charges, currency conversion, or partial payment.

Compare the invoice, client transfer confirmation, and receiving account statement.

If the client sent the full invoice amount but intermediary fees reduced the settlement, determine whether the contract explains who is responsible for those charges.

Avoid assuming that every shortfall is caused by an exchange-rate change.

## 8. Reduce Unnecessary Currency Conversions

Repeated conversions can create avoidable costs.

A freelancer who receives EUR, converts it into USD, and later converts USD into a local currency may incur more than one conversion charge.

Where supported and appropriate, consider whether the payment provider or bank allows funds to remain in the original currency.

However, holding multiple currencies introduces its own considerations, including account availability, exchange-rate exposure, and local financial rules.

The objective should be to simplify the payment route rather than accumulate currencies without a clear reason.

## 9. Separate Currency Management From Business Spending

The currency used to receive project revenue does not have to be the same as the payment method used for software subscriptions or online services.

For eligible business purchases, a separate card can help organize expenses and identify the currency charged by a merchant.

CBPayments offers card payment options for supported online transactions. Freelancers can review the platform at [CBPayments](https://www.cbpayments.cc/) and check its supported currencies, fees, and usage conditions.

A card is a spending tool; it should not be confused with a foreign-currency receiving account.

## Final Thoughts

Currency differences are a normal part of international freelance work, but unclear payment terms can turn them into unnecessary disputes.

Agree on the invoice currency before work begins, explain fee responsibilities, record the actual settlement details, and keep conversion records with the relevant invoice.

A clear process protects both the freelancer and the client.
