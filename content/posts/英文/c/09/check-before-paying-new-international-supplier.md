Working with international suppliers can help an online business access new products, services, and opportunities. However, paying a supplier in another country may involve additional uncertainty: an unfamiliar business, different payment practices, multiple currencies, and communication across time zones.

A low price or attractive offer should never be the only reason to send money. Before making the first payment, verify the supplier and understand exactly what you are paying for.

## Verify the Supplier's Identity

Confirm that the supplier is a real business or legitimate service provider. Check its official website, business name, contact details, and available registration information. Compare information across multiple sources rather than relying on a single profile or message.

If the supplier operates through a marketplace, review seller history, customer feedback, and dispute procedures. Reviews provide context but cannot guarantee reliability.

Be careful if the payment recipient has a different name from the company you are dealing with. There may be legitimate reasons, but ask for an explanation and verify it independently before proceeding.

## Confirm the Product or Service Details

Make sure both sides agree on what will be delivered. A written quotation or order confirmation should describe product or service specifications, quantity or scope, unit and total price, delivery method or completion date, shipping or service costs, warranty or support terms, and cancellation or refund conditions.

For digital services, clarify deliverables, access period, renewal terms, and limitations. Avoid vague descriptions such as “complete package.” Specific terms reduce misunderstandings.

## Review the Payment Instructions Carefully

Payment instructions should match the supplier's confirmed business information. Check the recipient name, account or card details, currency, amount, and invoice reference.

If payment instructions change shortly before sending money, verify the change through a previously established contact method. An unexpected request to use a new recipient or unfamiliar channel deserves additional scrutiny.

Do not send money simply because someone claims an order will be cancelled unless payment is made immediately. Pressure and artificial urgency are common warning signs.

## Understand the Payment Terms

Suppliers may request full payment in advance, a deposit, payment after delivery, or instalments. Understand what each payment covers and what happens if the supplier does not meet the agreed terms.

A deposit should have a clear purpose and amount. A staged arrangement should specify which milestone must be completed before the next payment is due.

Ask about accepted payment methods and fees. For international transactions, confirm who is responsible for transfer charges and currency conversion costs.

## Check Currency and Total Cost

The quoted price may not be the final amount your business spends. International payments can involve exchange-rate differences, service fees, intermediary charges, and receiving fees.

Calculate the expected total cost in your business's main currency. Keep a record of the original invoice currency and the amount actually charged or transferred. This makes supplier costs easier to compare later.

## Choose a Payment Method That Fits the Transaction

The method should suit the supplier's requirements and the documentation or protection you need. Depending on the transaction, options may include a bank transfer, supported card payment, or a payment service with a documented transaction history.

Do not assume a card payment is always reversible or a bank transfer can always be recovered. Dispute rights and refund procedures vary by provider, country, and transaction type.

## Keep Every Document

Save the quotation, invoice, order confirmation, payment receipt, and important messages. Use consistent file names so you can locate them later. Record promised delivery dates and important commitments.

If the order is delayed or incomplete, written records help establish what was agreed.

## Start With a Smaller Transaction When Appropriate

For a first-time supplier, consider a smaller initial order or limited trial if practical. This may help assess communication, quality, delivery performance, and payment procedures before committing to a larger order.

This may not be possible for customized production or services with substantial setup costs. In those cases, use a detailed written agreement and consider a suitable payment schedule.
