Payment providers may retain part of a merchant's funds to manage potential refunds, chargebacks, fraud losses, or other liabilities. Two terms that may appear in merchant agreements are rolling reserve and fixed reserve.

Both can reduce the amount immediately available to a merchant, but they do not necessarily work in the same way.

## What Is a Rolling Reserve?

A rolling reserve retains a percentage of eligible transactions for a defined period. For example, a provider may withhold 10% of eligible payments for 90 days. Amounts retained from earlier periods may become eligible for release while newer transactions continue to enter the reserve.

The balance changes over time as transactions are processed and older amounts reach their release dates.

## What Is a Fixed Reserve?

A fixed reserve generally requires a merchant to maintain a specified amount or target balance. An agreement might require a reserve balance of $5,000 and explain how it is funded, when it can be used, and under what conditions it may be released.

Some arrangements require an initial deposit; others build the balance through payout deductions until the target is reached. The term is not used identically by every provider, so read the contract's definition.

## Key Differences

A rolling reserve is usually calculated from transaction activity and released according to a time-based schedule. A fixed reserve is usually tied to a target balance.

Under a rolling model, the amount retained may rise or fall with eligible processing volume. Under a fixed model, the required balance may remain relatively stable once the target is reached, unless the agreement permits changes.

Neither arrangement guarantees that funds can be withdrawn on a particular date. Disputes, refunds, account termination, and other contractual conditions may affect release.

## A Simplified Example

If a merchant processes $20,000 and has a 10% rolling reserve, $2,000 may be retained from that eligible processing amount, subject to the agreement's calculation rules.

Under a fixed-reserve arrangement, the provider might require a $5,000 reserve balance. The amount deducted from a particular payout depends on how much has already been funded and the funding mechanism.

These examples are simplified; actual contracts may use thresholds, caps, different release periods, or additional conditions.

## Which Is More Predictable?

It depends on the business. A rolling reserve may be easier to estimate when volume is stable and the percentage and release period are clear. A growing business may see larger amounts withheld as sales increase.

A fixed reserve provides a visible target but may create a substantial initial or ongoing working-capital requirement. Model both arrangements using realistic sales, refunds, disputes, and payout schedules.

## What Should Merchants Compare?

Ask whether the reserve is percentage-based or balance-based, whether there is a cap, how long funds are retained, whether release is automatic or reviewed, whether terms can change, what deductions are permitted, and what happens when the account closes.

If reserves affect material working capital, obtain the terms in writing rather than relying on a verbal explanation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a merchant have both types?** An agreement may include multiple reserve mechanisms. It should explain how they interact.

**Is a fixed reserve always better for cash flow?** No. It may be more predictable, but the required balance can tie up substantial funds.

**Is a rolling reserve released automatically?** The agreement may define a schedule, but release can remain subject to outstanding liabilities and account status.
