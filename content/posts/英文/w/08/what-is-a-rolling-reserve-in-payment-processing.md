A merchant may process thousands of dollars in card payments but receive a smaller payout than expected. One possible reason is a rolling reserve.

A rolling reserve is a risk-management arrangement in which a provider withholds a portion of eligible transaction proceeds for a specified period. The retained amount may later become eligible for release under the merchant agreement, subject to permitted adjustments or claims.

## What Is a Rolling Reserve?

A rolling reserve retains a percentage of eligible transactions for a defined period. For example, an agreement might require 10% of eligible processing volume to be held for 90 days. Amounts retained from earlier periods may become eligible for release while newer transactions continue to contribute to the reserve.

The percentage, calculation basis, holding period, and release schedule must be confirmed in the contract. Some arrangements calculate the reserve from gross transactions; others apply different exclusions or netting rules.

## How Does It Work?

Suppose a merchant processes $10,000 in eligible transactions and has a 10% reserve requirement. The provider retains $1,000 before other fees or adjustments, leaving $9,000 before those other items.

After the applicable holding period, the retained $1,000 may become eligible for release if the agreement's conditions are met and no permitted deductions apply. This is an illustration, not a universal calculation method.

## Why Do Providers Use Rolling Reserves?

A reserve may provide funds against potential future liabilities such as refunds, chargebacks, fraud losses, unfulfilled orders, or other obligations covered by the agreement.

A reserve does not by itself prove that a merchant committed fraud or breached a rule. It may be a contractual risk measure based on the provider's assessment of the business.

## Rolling Reserve vs. Fixed Reserve

A rolling reserve is generally linked to transaction activity and a time-based release schedule. A fixed reserve generally refers to a specified amount or target balance that the merchant must maintain.

The terminology is not identical across providers. Merchants should examine how their agreement defines each reserve, how it is funded, and when funds may be released.

## What Should Merchants Check?

Review the reserve percentage or target, gross-versus-net calculation basis, holding period, release schedule, permitted deductions, whether terms can be changed, and what happens when the account is terminated. Also check whether reserve movements appear clearly in statements.

Pay particular attention to the provider's rights to increase or extend a reserve and any notice requirements.

## How Can a Merchant Reduce Reserve Pressure?

A merchant cannot assume good performance will automatically remove a reserve. It can, however, improve its risk profile through accurate product descriptions, realistic delivery estimates, transparent billing, responsive support, effective fraud controls, and prompt refund handling.

Monitor dispute trends and notify the provider before material changes to the business model or processing volume where required.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is reserve money lost?** Not necessarily. It is generally retained under agreed terms and may be released after the applicable period, subject to deductions and contractual conditions.

**Does every merchant have a rolling reserve?** No. Requirements vary by provider, business model, processing history, and risk assessment.

**Can a merchant negotiate the terms?** Some providers may review reserve terms based on performance or evidence. Any change should be confirmed in writing.

When comparing providers, review reserve terms alongside fees, payout timing, dispute charges, and support. Cardholder products such as [CBPayments](https://www.cbpayments.cc/) have separate terms and should not be confused with merchant reserve arrangements.
