You complete an online purchase for USD 50, but the amount later shown on your card statement is different. This can be confusing, especially when the merchant's confirmation email appears to show a fixed price. A difference does not automatically mean that the merchant charged you incorrectly. The final amount can be affected by currency conversion, fees, authorization adjustments, and additional charges.

## Currency conversion

A merchant may charge in a currency different from the billing currency of your card. If a website charges EUR 40 and your card account is denominated in USD, the transaction must be converted. The rate used for the completed transaction may differ from the rate shown by a search engine or currency calculator when you placed the order.

Exchange rates move, and the conversion methodology and timing depend on the issuer, payment network, provider, and transaction route. A public reference rate is useful for estimating a cost, but it is not a guaranteed statement rate.

## Foreign transaction fees

Some cards charge a foreign transaction fee for eligible international or foreign-currency purchases. The fee may be calculated as a percentage of the converted transaction amount, depending on the card agreement. For example, a hypothetical 3% fee on a USD 100 eligible transaction would add USD 3. Actual fees vary, and some cards do not charge one.

Check whether your card's terms define the fee by transaction currency, merchant location, processing location, or another criterion. A purchase displayed in your home currency may still be processed by an international merchant.

## Authorization holds and final settlement

An authorization is not always the final charge. Hotels, car rental companies, fuel stations, and restaurants may authorize an estimated amount or temporary hold before the final bill is known. The completed transaction may be lower or higher after the merchant submits the final amount.

A pending authorization and a posted transaction should therefore be treated differently. Check the transaction status before assuming that both amounts are separate completed charges.

## Taxes and additional charges

The initial product price may exclude sales tax, VAT, delivery, service charges, tips, customs duties, or optional extras. Compare the final invoice with the card statement, not just the price first displayed on the product page.

Subscriptions can also change after a trial or introductory period. A renewal may include a different plan, additional seats, regional taxes, or a price adjustment. Review the service's billing history if the amount differs from the previous month.

## A practical investigation

1. Find the final merchant invoice and identify its currency.
2. Check whether the card transaction is pending or posted.
3. Compare the original amount with the statement currency and converted amount.
4. Review the card agreement for foreign transaction or conversion fees.
5. Look for taxes, tips, delivery, deposits, or subscription changes.
6. Ask the merchant to explain the invoice amount and ask the issuer to explain conversion and issuer fees.

If the transaction is unauthorized or the final amount is genuinely incorrect, use the issuer's official dispute process promptly. Do not share passwords or one-time codes with anyone claiming to investigate a charge.

## How businesses can prevent confusion

A clear payment confirmation should identify the original amount, currency, taxes, additional fees, merchant descriptor, and refund terms. Businesses accepting international payments should explain which currency will be charged and retain transaction-level records.

## Final thoughts

A statement difference can be legitimate, but it should be explainable. Compare the invoice, transaction status, currency, and fee details before deciding what happened.
