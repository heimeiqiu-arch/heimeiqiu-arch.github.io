# What Is a Payment Aggregator?

A small business may want to accept online card payments without negotiating every part of the payment infrastructure independently. A **payment aggregator** can provide a shared service arrangement that allows multiple merchants to access payment acceptance through one provider's platform or acquiring relationships.

The term is used differently across markets and providers. It is therefore important to understand the actual contractual model rather than assuming every company using the label performs the same role. Businesses comparing payment tools can also review [CBPayments](https://www.cbpayments.cc/) as part of their broader payment research.

## What Is a Payment Aggregator?

A payment aggregator is generally a provider that brings payment acceptance services together for multiple businesses. It may enable merchants to accept payments through a shared platform, common technical integration, or an arrangement supported by one or more acquiring partners.

In some models, merchants operate under a master merchant relationship or another aggregated arrangement. In other markets, the term may describe a broader payment service provider. The legal structure, onboarding responsibilities, and settlement flow depend on local rules, network requirements, and the provider's contracts.

A payment aggregator is not automatically a bank or card network. It may rely on external acquirers, processors, gateways, and banking partners.

## How Does a Payment Aggregator Work?

A typical arrangement may involve these steps:

1. **Merchant application:** The business provides information about its ownership, products, location, and expected payment activity.
2. **Eligibility and risk review:** The provider reviews the business under its onboarding policies and applicable obligations.
3. **Technical setup:** The merchant integrates hosted checkout, an API, a payment link, or another supported payment interface.
4. **Customer transaction:** A buyer submits a payment, which is processed through the provider's connected payment infrastructure.
5. **Transaction records:** The platform records payments, refunds, fees, and other adjustments.
6. **Merchant payout:** Funds are distributed according to the contractual settlement and payout arrangement.

The sequence is simplified. Some aggregators provide direct merchant onboarding, while others work through partner platforms or acquiring institutions.

## Why Do Businesses Use Aggregators?

A shared service can reduce the technical work required to connect to payment infrastructure. Merchants may receive a common integration, consolidated reporting, payment methods, refund tools, and support from one provider.

This can be useful for small businesses, online platforms, marketplaces, and sellers that do not need to build their own acquiring connections. However, the convenience should be weighed against fees, payout terms, eligibility limits, and the merchant's level of control.

## Payment Aggregator vs. Payment Facilitator

The terms can overlap in everyday industry language, but they should not automatically be treated as interchangeable. A payment facilitator (PayFac) commonly refers to a more specific submerchant model within an acquiring arrangement, with defined responsibilities under applicable network and regulatory frameworks.

An aggregator may describe a wider range of shared payment acceptance models. Whether a particular aggregator is also operating as a PayFac depends on its actual structure, registrations, acquiring relationship, and responsibilities.

## What Should Merchants Check?

Before choosing an aggregator, review:

- **Contracting entity:** Identify the company that provides the service and the acquiring or banking partners involved.
- **Merchant status:** Understand whether you have an individual merchant account, a submerchant relationship, or another arrangement.
- **Fees:** Check percentage rates, fixed charges, monthly fees, cross-border costs, currency conversion, refund charges, and dispute fees.
- **Payout terms:** Confirm timing, minimum payout thresholds, reserves, holds, and payout currencies.
- **Risk and restrictions:** Review prohibited business types, verification requirements, dispute procedures, and termination conditions.
- **Data and support:** Understand reporting access, reconciliation exports, support hours, and responsibility for payment incidents.

Do not select a provider based solely on a low advertised rate. The actual cost and operational fit depend on your transaction profile and agreement.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a payment aggregator the same as a payment gateway?
No. A gateway typically provides technology for securely transmitting payment information. An aggregator describes a service or merchant-acceptance model. One provider may offer both.

### Does an aggregator hold merchant funds?
The funds flow depends on the provider's acquiring and payout arrangement. Review the contract to identify which entity receives, safeguards, and distributes funds.

### Can every business use an aggregator?
No. Providers may restrict certain industries, countries, products, and transaction patterns.
