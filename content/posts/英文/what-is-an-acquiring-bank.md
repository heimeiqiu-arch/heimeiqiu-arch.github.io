# What Is an Acquiring Bank? Understanding Merchant Acquiring

When you pay for something with a credit or debit card, several organizations may be involved in processing the transaction. The merchant, card issuer, card network, and acquiring institution each have different responsibilities.

One important participant is the **acquiring bank**, also known as an acquirer. It helps merchants accept card payments and connects them to the payment systems needed to process those transactions.

Understanding the role of an acquirer can help business owners evaluate payment providers, understand transaction fees, and troubleshoot payment acceptance problems.

## What Is an Acquiring Bank?

An acquiring bank is a financial institution that provides merchant acquiring services. It enables a business to accept eligible card payments and facilitates the processing of those transactions through relevant payment networks and partners.

Depending on the arrangement, an acquirer may provide or support:

- Merchant onboarding and underwriting.
- Access to card payment networks.
- Transaction authorization and processing connections.
- Settlement of funds to the merchant.
- Chargeback and dispute management.
- Merchant risk monitoring and compliance controls.

The exact responsibilities vary by provider and market. In some arrangements, a payment service provider or payment facilitator manages the merchant relationship while relying on a separate acquiring institution behind the scenes.

The term "acquiring bank" is widely used, but an acquirer does not have to perform every payment-related function itself.

## How Does an Acquiring Bank Work?

A simplified card payment involves several stages.

### Step 1: The customer initiates a payment

A customer enters card details or uses a supported digital wallet at checkout. The merchant's payment system sends the transaction request to its payment provider or gateway.

### Step 2: The transaction reaches the acquiring side

The payment request is routed through the merchant's acquiring arrangement and relevant card network toward the card issuer.

### Step 3: The issuer makes an authorization decision

The issuer evaluates the transaction using factors such as account status, available funds or credit, transaction details, and risk controls. It returns an approval or decline response.

### Step 4: The merchant receives the response

The merchant's checkout system displays the payment result. An approved authorization does not necessarily mean that the funds have already been transferred to the merchant.

### Step 5: Clearing and settlement take place

After the transaction is submitted for processing, the relevant institutions calculate and transfer amounts according to the applicable payment arrangements. The acquirer or its partners then facilitate payment of the merchant's proceeds, subject to fees, adjustments, reserves, and settlement terms.

Actual transaction flows vary by card network, region, payment type, and provider.

## Acquiring Bank vs. Issuing Bank

The acquiring bank and issuing bank participate on different sides of a card transaction.

| Factor | Acquiring Bank | Issuing Bank |
|---|---|---|
| Main relationship | Merchant | Cardholder |
| Primary role | Enables merchant card acceptance | Issues the payment card and manages the cardholder account |
| Transaction responsibility | Connects merchant transactions to payment systems | Evaluates authorization requests |
| Funds and settlement | Facilitates settlement to the merchant under the relevant arrangement | Handles the cardholder's account and applicable payment obligations |
| Common issue handled | Merchant onboarding, acquiring access, and merchant disputes | Card status, account restrictions, and cardholder authorization decisions |

For example, if a customer uses a card to buy software online, the issuing bank is the institution that issued the customer's card. The acquiring bank serves the merchant's card acceptance arrangement.

The two institutions may communicate through a card network, but they have different responsibilities.

## Acquiring Bank vs. Payment Gateway

A payment gateway is a technology service that securely transmits payment information between the merchant's checkout and the relevant payment infrastructure.

An acquiring bank provides acquiring services and access to merchant card acceptance. A gateway may connect to an acquirer, and some providers offer both gateway and acquiring services.

| Feature | Acquiring Bank | Payment Gateway |
|---|---|---|
| Primary function | Merchant acquiring and card acceptance | Payment data transmission and technical connectivity |
| Merchant onboarding | Often part of the acquiring arrangement | Depends on the provider |
| Authorization routing | Participates through its acquiring connections | Transmits requests through supported connections |
| Settlement | May facilitate settlement under the acquiring agreement | Does not necessarily provide acquiring or settlement services itself |
| Provider structure | Financial institution or eligible acquiring entity | Technology service that may connect to one or more acquirers |

These are general distinctions. Some payment companies combine gateway, processing, and acquiring functions in one service.

## What Is a Payment Facilitator?

A payment facilitator, often shortened to PayFac, is a service provider that enables eligible businesses to accept payments under a supported acquiring arrangement.

Instead of every small business independently establishing a traditional direct acquiring relationship, a payment facilitator may simplify onboarding and provide a shared payment platform.

The payment facilitator and acquiring bank are not the same thing. A PayFac generally operates within a defined arrangement with an acquirer and assumes responsibilities determined by its contracts, registration status, and applicable rules.

## How Do Acquiring Banks Make Money?

Acquirers may earn revenue through merchant service charges and other fees specified in their agreements.

The overall cost of accepting card payments can include several components:

- Interchange fees associated with eligible transactions.
- Card network assessment and other network charges.
- Acquirer processing or service fees.
- Gateway or payment service provider charges.
- Additional costs for particular transaction types or services.

These components should not be treated as identical. Interchange is generally associated with the issuer side of a card transaction, while an acquirer's revenue depends on the pricing structure and services provided.

A merchant may receive a blended rate that combines several costs, or an itemized pricing model that separates different fee components. Actual charges depend on the merchant's country, card type, transaction characteristics, and commercial agreement.

## What Happens When a Payment Is Declined?

An acquiring bank does not make every final authorization decision by itself. When a transaction is declined, the reason may relate to the issuer, cardholder account, merchant configuration, risk controls, card network, or a technical issue.

For example:

- The issuer may decline a transaction because of an account restriction.
- The merchant's acquiring setup may not support a particular transaction type.
- Incorrect payment data may prevent processing.
- A security rule may require additional verification.
- A technical issue may interrupt the transaction.

Merchants should use the available response information and contact the appropriate payment provider when the cause is unclear. Repeatedly submitting the same transaction without addressing the underlying issue may not help.

## How Do Acquiring Banks Handle Chargebacks?

A chargeback is a card payment dispute handled through the applicable payment network and financial institutions.

When a cardholder disputes a transaction, the issuer generally receives and evaluates the dispute under the relevant rules. The acquiring side communicates with the merchant, passes dispute information through the applicable process, and supports the merchant's response where permitted.

The merchant may need to provide evidence such as order records, delivery confirmation, customer communications, or transaction details.

The exact procedure, deadlines, fees, and allocation of responsibility depend on the card network, jurisdiction, and merchant agreement. An acquiring bank cannot guarantee that a merchant will win a dispute.

## How Should a Business Choose an Acquiring Provider?

Merchants should assess more than the headline transaction rate when comparing acquiring options.

### 1. Supported markets and payment methods

Confirm that the provider supports your business location, target customer markets, currencies, card networks, and transaction types.

### 2. Pricing transparency

Ask for a clear explanation of transaction fees, monthly charges, refunds, chargebacks, cross-border payments, and any reserve or settlement conditions.

### 3. Settlement timing

Understand when funds are normally paid out, which factors can delay settlement, and whether reserves or rolling holds may apply.

### 4. Risk and dispute support

Review onboarding requirements, fraud controls, dispute workflows, and the documentation merchants must provide.

### 5. Technical integration

Check whether the provider integrates with your website platform, payment gateway, reporting tools, and existing business systems.

### 6. Support and operational reliability

Evaluate support availability, incident communication, documentation, and the process for escalating payment issues.

A provider that offers a low advertised rate may not be the best option if it lacks the markets, technical features, or operational support the business needs.

## Do Acquiring Banks Matter to Virtual Card Users?

Yes, although cardholders do not usually select the merchant's acquiring bank directly.

A virtual card transaction still passes through the relevant merchant and payment infrastructure. Whether it succeeds may depend on the card issuer, card network, merchant's acquiring setup, transaction restrictions, authentication requirements, and other factors.

A virtual card is not guaranteed to work at every merchant. Users should confirm that the card supports the intended transaction and follow any required verification steps.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is an acquiring bank the same as a card network?

No. A card network provides the rules and infrastructure for routing and processing eligible card transactions. An acquiring bank provides merchant acquiring services and connects merchants to the relevant payment systems.

### Does the acquiring bank approve a card payment?

The issuer generally makes the card authorization decision. The acquiring side routes the request and participates in the transaction process, but approval also depends on the issuer and other applicable controls.

### Does every online merchant have its own acquiring bank?

Not necessarily. A merchant may work through a payment service provider or payment facilitator that uses an acquiring institution. The underlying arrangement depends on the provider and the merchant's contract.

### Does the acquiring bank transfer the full purchase amount to the merchant?

Not always. The merchant's payout may reflect processing fees, refunds, chargebacks, reserves, and other adjustments under the agreement. Settlement timing and net payout calculations vary by provider.

### Can changing acquiring banks improve payment acceptance?

It may help in certain situations, such as when a business needs better regional coverage or a more suitable payment setup. However, changing acquirers does not guarantee higher approval rates because issuer decisions, customer behavior, card details, and other factors also matter.

## Final Thoughts

An acquiring bank helps merchants accept card payments by connecting them to the relevant payment infrastructure and facilitating the merchant side of transaction processing.

It differs from an issuing bank, which manages the cardholder's account and evaluates authorization requests, and from a payment gateway, which provides technical connectivity.

For merchants, understanding these roles makes it easier to compare providers, evaluate fees, and investigate payment problems.
