A card skimming attack is a type of payment fraud in which criminals capture payment card information without the cardholder's knowledge.

Skimming is commonly associated with physical payment terminals, ATMs and fuel pumps. Criminals may install unauthorised devices or compromise legitimate equipment to collect card data. Stolen information may then be used to create counterfeit cards or support other forms of payment fraud, depending on the data obtained and the security features involved.

Although chip technology has made certain forms of card cloning more difficult, skimming remains a concern in some environments.

## How card skimming works

Traditional skimming often involves a device placed over or inside a legitimate card reader. When a customer inserts or swipes a card, the device may capture information from the magnetic stripe.

Criminals may also try to capture a PIN through a hidden camera or an unauthorised keypad overlay. Not every skimming device works in the same way. Some target magnetic-stripe information, while more sophisticated attacks may involve tampering with payment terminals or exploiting other weaknesses.

## Where skimming can happen

Potentially vulnerable locations include ATMs, fuel station payment terminals, unattended vending machines, self-service kiosks, retail payment terminals and other card readers with limited physical supervision.

The presence of a card reader in one of these locations does not mean it has been compromised. Risk depends on equipment security, inspection practices and the surrounding environment.

## Warning signs

Be cautious if a terminal appears physically unusual. Possible warning signs include loose or misaligned components, damaged seals, unusual attachments around the card slot or a keypad that appears different from nearby machines.

If a terminal looks tampered with, avoid using it and notify the business or operator. At ATMs, check for unusual devices around the card slot and keypad. Shield the keypad when entering a PIN.

## How consumers can reduce risk

Where available, use contactless or chip-based transactions rather than magnetic-stripe swipes. Inspect unfamiliar payment terminals before inserting a card. Shield your PIN even if nobody appears nearby.

Enable transaction alerts so unfamiliar purchases can be identified quickly. If you suspect your card information has been captured, contact the issuer and ask about blocking or replacing the card.

## How merchants can prevent skimming

Merchants should use approved payment terminals and follow equipment providers' installation and inspection procedures. Staff should know how to identify suspicious attachments or signs of tampering.

Physical access to payment terminals should be controlled, particularly for unattended equipment. Merchants should maintain secure payment systems, keep terminal software updated and follow relevant payment security requirements.

## Does EMV chip technology stop skimming?

EMV chip technology provides stronger protection than traditional magnetic-stripe transactions because chip transactions use dynamic authentication data.

However, chip technology does not eliminate every payment fraud risk. Some transactions may fall back to magnetic-stripe processing, and criminals may use stolen information in online transactions or other fraud scenarios.

Contactless and mobile wallet transactions use different security mechanisms, so their risk profile is not identical to traditional magnetic-stripe payments.

## Final thoughts

Card skimming involves unauthorised capture of payment card information, often through tampered physical terminals. Consumers can reduce risk by inspecting payment devices, shielding PIN entry and monitoring transactions. Merchants should protect terminals through regular inspections, access controls and secure maintenance practices.
