A Merchant Category Code, or MCC, is a four-digit code used to classify a merchant according to its primary business activity. Card networks and payment providers use these codes in processing, reporting, risk management, and some spending-control programs.

## What an MCC identifies
An MCC describes the merchant's category, not the exact item purchased or the cardholder's purpose. A restaurant category does not reveal whether a meal was a business lunch or a personal purchase. Codes are generally assigned through the merchant's acquiring and payment-processing arrangements under network rules.

## Why it matters for business cards
Some card programs allow administrators to permit or block categories. A company might allow travel and office-supply spending while restricting categories unrelated to its expense policy. These rules can reduce inappropriate use, but broad restrictions may also block legitimate merchants. A category can contain businesses with different models, and an allowed category does not make every purchase appropriate.

## How restrictions cause declines
A payment may be declined because its merchant category is restricted even when the card has funds and the amount is below the spending cap. Increasing the limit may not solve a category restriction. Ask the issuer whether the merchant category applied and request an approved exception if appropriate.

## MCCs are not merchant names
A statement descriptor may show a parent company, marketplace, or payment facilitator rather than the familiar brand. MCC is separate data. When investigating a payment, review descriptor, date, amount, receipt, invoice, and category if available. Do not label a transaction fraudulent solely because the category looks unfamiliar.

Finance teams can use MCC data to review category spending and investigate repeated declines. Treat these as signals, not proof. An MCC is useful context, but it should be considered alongside the actual merchant, purchase evidence, and business purpose.
