A business card policy that gives every employee the same spending limit may seem simple, but it rarely reflects how different teams work. Marketing may pay for advertising and design tools, sales may cover travel, and operations may buy supplies or logistics services. The objective is not to restrict every payment. It is to give employees enough flexibility while keeping spending visible and accountable.

## Map spending to team responsibilities

Start by reviewing recent card statements, invoices, reimbursement claims, and recurring subscriptions. Group expenses by business purpose: marketing platforms, travel, cloud services, office supplies, supplier payments, and project costs. Identify which expenses are predictable, which fluctuate, and which require advance approval. Do not assume every person in a department needs identical access; a manager, routine purchaser, and temporary contractor have different responsibilities.

## Combine several controls

A per-transaction limit controls the size of an individual purchase. A daily limit restricts cumulative spending during a defined period. A monthly budget helps departments stay within their allocation. Where supported, merchant or category restrictions can limit certain types of purchases. Temporary permissions can cover a one-off equipment purchase or business trip without permanently raising an employee's allowance.

These controls solve different problems. A payment can be below the transaction limit but exceed the remaining daily budget. A card can have sufficient funds and still be blocked by a merchant restriction. Confirm which controls the provider actually offers before designing a policy around them.

## Set limits using evidence

Use several months of transaction history to estimate normal spending. For example, if a marketing team consistently spends around $1,800 each month on approved services, set a realistic monthly budget with a modest buffer for expected variation. Keep a separate transaction cap so one unexpected purchase cannot consume the entire allowance. For occasional travel, approve a temporary trip budget with a purpose and end date rather than permanently increasing the card limit.

## Assign ownership and review exceptions

Every card should have a named owner, department, business purpose, approved limits, permitted expense types, and review frequency. Shared cards make accountability harder; if one is necessary, require a transaction log identifying the user and purchase purpose. Review unusual merchants, repeated near-limit transactions, unexpected renewals, and sudden spending increases. Treat an alert as a reason to ask questions, not as proof of misuse.

## Revisit the policy

Review controls when teams grow, roles change, new suppliers are introduced, or the company enters a new market. Quarterly reviews are a useful starting point, with more frequent checks for higher-risk activity. Businesses evaluating virtual or physical cards, including CBPayments, should verify available controls, fees, funding arrangements, merchant acceptance, and transaction terms before assigning cards to staff.

## Final thoughts

Effective spending controls reflect real business responsibilities. Combine transaction caps, budgets, merchant rules, and approval procedures; give each card a clear owner; and update settings as spending patterns change. A good system makes legitimate purchases straightforward and unusual activity easier to investigate.
