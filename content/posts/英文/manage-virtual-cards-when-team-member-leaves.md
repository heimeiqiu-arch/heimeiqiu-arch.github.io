# How to Manage Virtual Cards When a Team Member Leaves

When an employee or contractor leaves a business, removing their access to company systems is only part of the offboarding process. If they were responsible for software subscriptions, advertising accounts, cloud services, or other online purchases, their payment methods also need attention.

A virtual card assigned to a team member may still be connected to recurring charges, stored in merchant accounts, or used for business services that other colleagues depend on. Closing everything immediately without checking these connections can interrupt important services. Leaving every card active, on the other hand, can create unnecessary financial and security risks.

A clear virtual card offboarding process helps businesses protect payment credentials while keeping essential operations running.

## Why virtual card offboarding matters

Businesses often use multiple cards to separate spending by department, employee, project, or service. This makes transactions easier to identify, but it also creates a responsibility to review card access when someone's role changes.

If a departing team member's card remains active, several problems may arise:

- A former employee may retain access to a payment method that is no longer needed.
- A software subscription may renew without an active internal owner.
- A business may lose track of which merchant is charging a particular card.
- A replacement payment method may be needed to prevent an interruption.
- Finance teams may have difficulty reconciling transactions after the employee leaves.

The goal is not simply to deactivate a card. It is to establish which payments should continue, which should stop, and who should manage them going forward.

## Step 1: Build an inventory of assigned cards

Start by identifying every virtual card associated with the departing employee or contractor.

Depending on your card management system, this may include cards issued specifically to that person, cards assigned to a department they managed, or cards stored in merchant accounts they administered.

For each card, record:

- The card's internal label or identifier.
- The employee, contractor, team, or project associated with it.
- Its current status and spending limits.
- Recent transactions and recurring payment activity.
- The merchants or services using the card, where known.
- The business owner responsible for each payment.

Avoid recording unnecessary sensitive card details in a general spreadsheet. Use internal identifiers and the card provider's secure management interface whenever possible.

If a card's purpose is unclear, review transaction descriptions, invoices, subscription dashboards, and accounting records before deciding what to do with it.

## Step 2: Identify subscriptions and business-critical payments

A card may pay for much more than the service its original holder used every day.

For example, a team member might have registered a card for a design platform, cloud storage, a project management tool, a domain renewal, or a marketing service. Other employees may still rely on those accounts.

Classify each payment into one of three groups:

**Continue:** The service is still required and needs a new internal owner or payment method.

**Cancel:** The service is no longer needed and should be cancelled through the merchant's official process.

**Investigate:** The purpose, owner, renewal date, or business value is not clear enough to make a decision.

Check renewal dates before changing cards. A service may have a monthly billing cycle, an annual renewal, or usage-based charges that are not obvious from the latest transaction.

For business-critical services, confirm that another authorized employee can access the merchant account before changing the payment method.

## Step 3: Decide whether to freeze, replace, or close each card

Not every card should receive the same treatment.

### Freeze the card when immediate control is needed

Freezing or temporarily locking a card may be appropriate while the business reviews its transactions and identifies active subscriptions. It can also be useful when the company needs to prevent new spending while investigating an unclear payment.

However, freezing a card may interrupt legitimate recurring charges. Check the provider's rules to understand how frozen cards affect existing authorizations and future transactions.

### Replace the card when the payment must continue

If a card is associated with an important service, the business may need to arrange a new authorized payment method.

Update the merchant's billing settings using an account controlled by the business. Confirm that the new payment method works before closing the old one, when the circumstances and provider rules allow this sequence.

Do not assume that issuing a replacement card automatically updates every merchant account.

### Close the card when it is no longer required

Once the business has confirmed that the card is no longer needed, closing it may reduce unnecessary exposure and simplify future reviews.

Before closure, check for pending transactions, unresolved refunds, recurring charges, and other activity that may require follow-up. A card's closure may not automatically cancel a merchant subscription or settle an existing transaction.

The correct action depends on the card provider's features, the company's security policy, and the operational needs associated with the card.

## Step 4: Transfer ownership of merchant accounts

Changing a payment method does not transfer ownership of a merchant account.

If a departing employee used a personal work email or was the only administrator of a service, the business should review account ownership and access permissions separately.

Where possible:

1. Assign an authorized internal owner.
2. Update account recovery and business contact details.
3. Remove the departing person's access according to company policy.
4. Review stored payment methods.
5. Confirm that billing notifications reach an active team member.
6. Store invoices and relevant business records in an approved location.

Do not rely on a former employee's personal inbox to manage future renewals or billing problems.

## Step 5: Review pending payments and refunds

Offboarding can coincide with transactions that have not fully settled.

A card may have pending authorizations, a refund may still be processing, or a merchant may have submitted a charge shortly before the card was frozen or closed.

Record unresolved items before completing the offboarding checklist. Include the merchant, amount, date, current status, and the person responsible for follow-up.

If a refund is expected, confirm how the provider handles refunds sent to a frozen, replaced, or closed card. Do not assume that closing a card cancels a refund or that every refund will automatically be redirected.

For unclear transactions, contact the merchant or card provider through an official support channel.

## Step 6: Keep an audit trail

A reliable offboarding record should explain what happened to each card and why.

Record the decision, the date it was made, the person who approved it, and any related subscription or account changes. Keep supporting invoices and transaction records according to the company's retention policy.

This information can help finance teams reconcile the final transactions, explain spending changes, and demonstrate that payment access was reviewed.

A simple process is often enough for a small team, while larger businesses may need formal approvals and separation of duties.

## How virtual cards can simplify the process

Virtual cards can make payment management more flexible when a business uses distinct cards for different employees, projects, or expense categories. A card with a clear internal label and defined spending limit is generally easier to review than one shared informally across multiple services.

The benefits depend on how the cards are configured and managed. A card alone does not provide complete visibility into merchant account ownership, subscription terms, or employee access.

If you use a platform such as [CBPayments](https://www.cbpayments.cc/), review the available card controls and transaction information to determine how they fit your company's offboarding process. Confirm which actions are supported by the provider rather than assuming that every card can be frozen, replaced, or closed in the same way.

## A practical offboarding checklist

Before completing an employee's payment offboarding, confirm that you have:

- Identified the cards associated with the departing person.
- Reviewed recent transactions and recurring charges.
- Assigned owners to services that must continue.
- Cancelled subscriptions that are no longer required.
- Updated payment methods for essential services where necessary.
- Reviewed pending transactions and refunds.
- Removed or transferred relevant merchant account access.
- Documented the decisions and outstanding follow-up actions.

## Final thoughts

Managing virtual cards when a team member leaves requires more than switching off a payment method. Businesses should first identify the cards, review their associated charges, transfer ownership of important services, and then decide whether each card should be frozen, replaced, or closed.

A consistent offboarding checklist reduces unnecessary spending and helps prevent disruptions to business operations. Most importantly, it gives the remaining team a clear record of which payments continue and who is responsible for them.
