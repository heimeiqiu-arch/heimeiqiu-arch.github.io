Month-end payment reconciliation is the process of confirming that payment activity for an accounting period is complete, supported, and accurately reflected in the business's financial records.

It is more than checking whether the total sales figure matches the bank balance. Customer payments, refunds, processing fees, chargebacks, settlement timing, and outstanding payouts all need to be considered.

A structured process helps finance teams close the month with fewer unexplained differences.

## Step 1: Define the reporting period

Before exporting reports, confirm the start and end dates of the accounting period. Identify which date fields the business uses for its reporting policy.

Payment systems may distinguish between transaction date, authorization date, capture date, settlement date, and payout date. These fields can place the same payment into different reporting periods.

Document the cutoff rules and apply them consistently.

## Step 2: Collect source reports

Gather the payment transaction report, capture and refund report, chargeback or dispute report, processor fee and adjustment report, payout or settlement report, bank statement, and internal accounting or sales ledger.

Keep the original exports in a controlled folder. Record when each report was generated and which period it covers.

## Step 3: Verify transaction totals

Group captured transactions by currency and relevant payment channel. Compare the processor totals with the internal sales or payment records.

Investigate missing transactions, duplicate imports, failed captures, and amount differences. Do not include authorization-only events as completed collections unless the business's accounting policy specifically requires a separate treatment.

## Step 4: Reconcile refunds and chargebacks

Match each refund to its original payment where possible. Confirm whether it was completed, pending, or failed.

Review chargebacks and dispute adjustments separately. These may affect settlement and accounting in ways that are different from ordinary refunds.

Pay attention to refunds issued after the original sale period. Keep the event dates and original references so that the accounting treatment can be reviewed properly.

## Step 5: Verify fees and adjustments

Compare processing fees with the provider's statement. Check whether fees were deducted from payouts or charged separately.

Review other adjustments, including reserves, corrections, and dispute-related amounts where applicable. Each material adjustment should have a supporting reference and explanation.

## Step 6: Match payouts to bank deposits

Group the processor's payout records by settlement reference. Calculate the expected net payout using captured payments, refunds, fees, and documented adjustments.

Match each payout to the corresponding bank deposit. If a payout has not reached the bank by the month-end cutoff, record it as outstanding according to the business's accounting policy rather than marking it as missing.

## Step 7: Review open exceptions

Prepare a list of all unmatched or partially matched items. Each item should include transaction or payout reference, amount and currency, date, reason for the difference, responsible owner, next action, and current status.

Prioritize material amounts, older exceptions, and items that may affect financial reporting.

## Step 8: Document the close

Once reconciliation is complete, save the final reports, matching results, adjustment records, and exception list. Record who prepared the reconciliation and who reviewed it.

A reviewer should be able to follow the trail from a reported payment total to the individual transactions and then to the settlement and bank records.

## A simple month-end example

Assume a business records $20,000 in captured card payments during the month. It has $600 in refunds, $400 in processing fees, and $100 in documented adjustments.

The expected net payout from those items is $20,000 − $600 − $400 + $100 = $19,100.

This is an illustrative calculation. The actual month-end bank deposits may differ because some transactions or refunds settle in another period. The reconciliation should identify those timing differences rather than forcing all activity into one deposit total.

## Common month-end mistakes

The most frequent problems include using gross sales as the expected bank deposit, overlooking refunds issued in a later period, mixing currencies, counting authorization events as completed payments, and leaving old unmatched items unresolved.

Another mistake is making manual adjustments without retaining the original report or documenting the reason. This weakens the audit trail and makes future reviews harder.

## Make month-end easier throughout the month

A month-end close should confirm work already performed, not be the first time anyone reviews payment activity. Regular matching, consistent file naming, and prompt exception handling reduce the workload at the end of each period.

Businesses using card-based payment methods for online services and operating expenses should also retain their statements and transaction records. CBPayments offers card options for online payments, while each business should reconcile those transactions against the actual records supplied by the relevant provider.

A strong month-end reconciliation gives management a clear view of what was collected, what was refunded, what was deducted, what reached the bank, and what still needs attention.
