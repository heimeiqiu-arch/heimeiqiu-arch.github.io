# How to Build a Payment Security Checklist for Small Businesses

Small businesses may handle payments through business cards, online gateways, merchant accounts, subscription platforms, and banking applications. As a business grows, access may be shared among employees, contractors, and external service providers. Without clear procedures, it becomes difficult to know who can approve transactions or change payment settings.

A practical checklist helps business owners organize responsibilities and identify gaps before they lead to financial losses.

## 1. Inventory every payment system

Create a list of business bank accounts, card issuer applications, merchant gateways, checkout platforms, subscription services, expense tools, and supplier-payment systems.

For each service, record its purpose, administrator, authorized users, and official support contact. Store the inventory securely and update it whenever a service is added or removed.

## 2. Review employee permissions

Use individual logins wherever supported. Assign access according to job responsibilities rather than convenience. A staff member who needs to view payment records may not need permission to transfer funds or change settlement information.

Review who has access, what each person can do, whether access remains necessary, when it was last checked, and who approved it. Remove access promptly when employees leave or change roles.

## 3. Strengthen authentication

Require unique passwords and enable multi-factor authentication where available. Prioritize administrator accounts, business email, banking platforms, and services that control money movement.

Use a password manager to reduce password reuse. Keep recovery details accurate and protected because a compromised email account can create a path into several payment services.

## 4. Set business-card rules

If employees use company cards, document which purchases are allowed, which expenses require approval, how receipts must be submitted, how limits are assigned, what to do when a card is lost, and how personal and business expenses are separated.

Where supported, match card controls to employee responsibilities. A person who buys approved software may not need the same allowance as someone responsible for travel or operations.

## 5. Monitor transactions and account changes

Review unfamiliar merchants, duplicate charges, unexpected recurring payments, unusual amounts, unrecognized refunds, settlement changes, and new administrator permissions. Enable transaction notifications where available.

Do not wait until month-end to identify every issue. Regular reviews make it easier to investigate while records are readily available.

## 6. Verify supplier payment changes

Payment redirection fraud may involve an attacker impersonating a supplier and requesting a bank-account change. Require staff to verify such requests through a previously established contact channel, not solely through the email that contained the request.

For significant changes, require a second authorized employee to approve. Record the request, verification, approval, and date.

## 7. Protect customer payment information

Collect and retain only information the business genuinely needs. Use established payment processors and follow their security requirements. Avoid storing full card details in spreadsheets, ordinary documents, email, or messaging applications.

Restrict access to payment records and understand the security obligations associated with your processing arrangements, including applicable PCI DSS requirements. Using an external payment provider does not remove every responsibility for protecting customer information.

## 8. Create an incident response plan

Assign responsibility for contacting providers, freezing affected cards or accounts, disabling compromised access, reviewing unauthorized transactions, securing associated email accounts, preserving evidence, informing management, and coordinating customer communication when necessary.

Keep support contacts accessible to authorized staff. After an incident, identify the cause and update procedures so the same weakness is less likely to recur.

## 9. Review subscriptions and unused accounts

Maintain a list of subscriptions, renewal dates, billing amounts, and responsible employees. Cancel services that are no longer needed through the official merchant process. Review inactive payment accounts and remove unnecessary users or saved payment methods.

## 10. Schedule regular reviews

A monthly review can include employee permissions, payment activity, card limits, subscription renewals, account-security settings, unused payment methods, supplier details, and unresolved incidents. Higher-volume businesses may need more frequent checks.

## Small-business payment security checklist

- [ ] Every payment system has an assigned administrator.
- [ ] Employees use individual accounts where supported.
- [ ] MFA is enabled where available.
- [ ] Passwords are unique and securely stored.
- [ ] Permissions match job responsibilities.
- [ ] Business-card rules are documented.
- [ ] Spending controls are reviewed.
- [ ] Transaction notifications are enabled where available.
- [ ] Supplier payment changes require independent verification.
- [ ] Customer card information is not stored unnecessarily.
- [ ] Recurring payments are reviewed.
- [ ] Unused accounts and payment methods are removed.
- [ ] An incident response procedure is documented.
- [ ] Security reviews have an assigned owner.

## Final thoughts

Small businesses do not need an overly complicated security program to improve payment protection. They need clear ownership, appropriate permissions, regular transaction reviews, and consistent procedures. Start with the accounts and cards that control the most important financial activities, then update the checklist as the business grows.
