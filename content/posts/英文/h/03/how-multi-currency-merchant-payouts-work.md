Multi-currency merchant payouts allow businesses to receive payment proceeds in different currencies. This is useful for companies selling internationally, but the currency shown at checkout is not necessarily the currency that reaches the merchant's bank account.

The provider may apply settlement rules, currency conversion, fees, and banking requirements before completing the payout.

## The Three Important Currency Stages

**Transaction currency** is the currency in which the customer pays. For example, a customer may purchase a product for 100 EUR.

**Settlement currency** is the currency in which the provider calculates or settles the merchant's transaction proceeds. The merchant may be able to retain the original currency or use another supported currency.

**Payout currency** is the currency in which the provider transfers funds to the merchant's receiving account. Settlement and payout currencies may be identical or different.

## How Does Currency Conversion Affect the Payout?

When conversion is required, the final amount depends on the applied exchange rate and fees.

Converted amount = Original amount × Applied exchange rate.

Estimated net payout = Converted amount − Conversion charges − Transfer fees − Other applicable deductions.

Actual calculations may be more complicated. Providers may use different rate sources, apply markups, or calculate fees at different stages.

## A Practical Conversion Example

Imagine a merchant has 1,000 EUR in eligible proceeds. For illustration, assume the provider applies an exchange rate of 1 EUR = 1.08 USD.

The converted gross amount is 1,000 × 1.08 = 1,080 USD.

Now assume a conversion fee of 1%, equal to 10.80 USD, and a transfer fee of 5 USD.

The estimated payout is 1,080 − 10.80 − 5 = 1,064.20 USD.

These are hypothetical rates and fees. Actual provider rates, fee calculations, and intermediary bank deductions may differ.

## Why Does Conversion Timing Matter?

Exchange rates change over time. A provider may convert a transaction when it is processed, when it becomes eligible for settlement, or when the payout is initiated. The exact timing depends on its rules.

A merchant may receive a customer payment on Monday, while conversion occurs in a later settlement cycle. If the exchange rate changes before conversion, the final payout may differ from an estimate made on the transaction date.

Check which rate source is used, when conversion occurs, whether a markup applies, whether the rate is fixed or variable, and whether the applied rate appears in the statement.

## Other Fees That Can Affect Cross-Border Payouts

Currency conversion is only one possible cost. Other charges may include international transfer fees, intermediary bank fees, receiving bank fees, and account-specific charges. Not every transfer incurs all these costs, so review the complete fee structure.

## Common Multi-Currency Payout Problems

**Unexpected payout amount:** The merchant estimated using the checkout currency rather than the provider's actual conversion rate.

**Unsupported receiving currency:** A bank may reject or convert an incoming currency depending on account rules.

**Refund currency differences:** A refund may use a different exchange rate from the original transaction, creating a reporting difference.

**Accounting mismatches:** Gross sales in one currency may be compared with net payouts in another without accounting for conversion and fees.

## How Can Businesses Reduce Currency Costs?

Identify which currencies the business actually needs. If the company regularly pays suppliers in USD, retaining eligible USD proceeds may reduce unnecessary conversion where supported.

Compare the total cost of receiving and converting funds across providers. Review the applied exchange rate and final bank credit, not just the headline processing fee.

Finance teams should retain the original transaction amount, settlement amount, exchange rate, conversion fee, payout amount, and bank credit.

## Should a Merchant Hold Foreign Currency or Convert Immediately?

Holding foreign currency can be useful when the business has future expenses in that currency, but it introduces exchange-rate exposure and requires a suitable account arrangement.

Converting immediately may simplify reporting and reduce currency exposure, but it can involve conversion costs. The right approach depends on transaction volume, operating expenses, and the company's ability to manage currency risk.

## Final Thoughts

Multi-currency payouts introduce variables beyond ordinary merchant funding. Understanding transaction currency, settlement currency, payout currency, conversion timing, and cross-border fees helps businesses estimate what they will receive.
