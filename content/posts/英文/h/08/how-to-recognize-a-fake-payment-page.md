# How to Recognize a Fake Payment Page

A payment page is often the last step before an online purchase. It may request a card number, expiry date, security code, billing details, or an additional verification code. Criminals imitate genuine checkout screens because customers are focused on completing a transaction and may not inspect the destination carefully.

## Examine the domain, not just the logo

Check the main website address before entering information. Scammers may add words, change a character, use a misleading subdomain, or place a familiar brand name inside an unrelated domain. The presence of a company name somewhere in the address does not establish that the page belongs to that company.

If you are uncertain, close the page and navigate to the merchant's official website independently. Be particularly careful with shortened links and payment URLs sent unexpectedly by text, email, or social media.

## Understand the HTTPS limitation

HTTPS encrypts the connection between your browser and the website. It does not confirm that the merchant is genuine. Fraudulent websites can also use HTTPS certificates, so a padlock symbol should never be your only test of legitimacy.

## Treat unexpected redirects carefully

Legitimate merchants sometimes use external payment processors. A redirect is therefore not automatically a scam. The important question is whether the destination is consistent with the merchant's stated payment process.

If a familiar retailer suddenly sends you to an unrelated domain that asks for card details, stop and verify the change through contact information found independently on the merchant's official site.

## Watch for excessive or unrelated requests

A normal card checkout may ask for card details, billing information, and additional authentication required by the issuer. Be suspicious if the page asks for your email password, banking login password, account recovery codes, remote access to your device, or information unrelated to the purchase.

Never tell a person your one-time password. Enter a code only into the genuine authentication process for a transaction you initiated and have checked. Read the verification message to confirm the merchant, amount, and action being authorized.

## Recognize pressure tactics

Fake payment pages are often distributed through messages claiming that an account will be closed, a package will be returned, or a special offer will expire unless payment is made immediately. Urgency is not proof of fraud, but it is a reason to slow down.

Open the relevant service independently and check whether the claimed problem exists. Do not use the link in the alarming message as your only route to the account.

## What to do if you entered card details

Contact your card issuer promptly using its official application or phone number. Explain what happened and ask whether the card should be frozen or replaced. Review pending and completed transactions, secure the associated shopping account, change reused passwords, and preserve the suspicious URL and messages.

If you also shared a verification code or account password, tell the issuer or provider. That information may change the response they recommend.

## A safer checkout routine

Before paying, start from the merchant's official website, verify the domain, check the amount and currency, and confirm the payment recipient where shown. Do not share OTPs with another person. Keep transaction alerts enabled and retain receipts.

## Final thoughts

A familiar logo, polished design, or HTTPS padlock is not enough to establish that a payment page is genuine. Verify the domain and the payment flow independently, and stop whenever a request does not make sense. A short pause before checkout is far less costly than trying to recover from stolen payment credentials.
