When an online business is run by more than one person, payment responsibilities often become shared. One person manages advertising, another pays for software, and someone else handles supplier orders.

This can save time, but it also creates a management challenge: who is allowed to make payments, which transactions need approval, and how can the business identify who made a purchase?

Without clear rules, teams may share passwords informally, make duplicate purchases, or leave payment access active after responsibilities change.

## Identify Who Needs Payment Access

List the people who handle business purchases or financial tasks. Not everyone who works for the business needs access to payment tools. A content writer may need a software account but not permission to change billing information. An advertising manager may need to review campaign spending but not supplier payment details.

Separate responsibilities into requesting a purchase, reviewing or approving it, completing payment, managing billing information, reviewing transaction records, and handling refunds or disputes. Give each person only the access required for their role.

## Avoid Sharing Login Credentials

Sharing one username and password may seem convenient for a small team, but it creates risks. You may not know who made a transaction, password changes can interrupt everyone, and former team members may retain access.

Where a service supports individual user accounts, use them. Enable appropriate authentication and avoid sending passwords or one-time codes through ordinary chat messages.

If a provider does not support multiple users, establish a controlled process for the account owner to complete approved requests.

## Set Clear Payment Approval Rules

A business does not need a complicated approval system for every small purchase. Define rules based on amount and purpose.

Routine subscriptions that have already been approved may be handled by an assigned team member. New services, unusual purchases, and larger expenses may require owner approval.

Write down the rules. An approval record can include the request date, purpose, expected amount, requester, and approving person. This reduces misunderstandings and helps investigate unexpected expenses.

## Use Separate Payment Methods for Different Purposes

When several people manage different parts of a business, payment separation can improve visibility. A business may organize spending by department, project, or expense category. A dedicated payment method for eligible online purchases may make certain transactions easier to identify.

## Keep a Record of Every Payment Request

A payment request should explain why the purchase is necessary. Record the service or supplier, expected amount, business purpose, requester, and approval status.

For recurring purchases, include billing frequency and renewal date. For one-time purchases, save the quotation or invoice. This is especially useful for remote teams working across time zones because a clear record reduces the need to search through old conversations.

## Review Access When Team Roles Change

Access management is not a one-time task. When someone changes roles, leaves the business, or completes a temporary project, review their access to payment platforms, billing accounts, and related services.

Remove permissions that are no longer needed. Update passwords or authentication settings when appropriate, especially if credentials may have been shared. Also review recovery email addresses, phone numbers, and administrator accounts.

## Monitor Transaction Notifications

Where supported, enable alerts for relevant transactions, failed payments, and account changes. Decide who should receive notifications and who is responsible for reviewing them.

Too many alerts can become noise, so focus on notifications that require action. If a transaction appears unfamiliar, verify it through the provider's official account interface rather than relying solely on an email or message.

## Create a Simple Monthly Access Review

Once a month, check who can make or approve payments and confirm that each person still needs their access. Review recent transactions for purchases without a clear request or business purpose. Also check recurring subscriptions and remove payment access from accounts no longer used.

## Balance Control With Convenience

The purpose of access management is not to prevent employees from doing their jobs. Start with individual accounts where available, limited permissions, simple approval rules, and regular reviews. Add detail as the business grows.
